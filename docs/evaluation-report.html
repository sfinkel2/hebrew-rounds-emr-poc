Does the system write correct medical notes?

We recorded a real 9-minute surgical ward round covering four patients, ran it through the system, and checked every fact it produced against an answer key written by hand.

Recording c853a222 · 7 Aug 2026 · 540 seconds · four patients · spoken Hebrew
Tested on both AI models the system supports: Claude Opus 4.8 (the default) and Claude Haiku 4.5 (about 5× cheaper)

The short version

Three things this test established.

1. The patient-splitting step works, and it is essential.

The system now cuts a ward round into one section per patient before it writes anything. With that step switched on, both models produced four clean, separate charts with zero mix-ups. With it switched off, both models failed badly — see point 2.

2. Without splitting, the two models fail in opposite ways — and both failures are invisible.

The cheap model blended all four patients into one chart: one patient's morphine and another's fever landed in the same record, and 19 of 33 entries were silently overwritten.

The expensive model did something arguably worse. It wrote a tidy, well-evidenced chart for the first patient and silently forgot the other three. Rachel and Michael simply do not appear. Nothing in the resulting note signals that anyone is missing — it looks complete.

3. Both models quietly "corrected" things the doctors never said.

The audio transcription is imperfect. Where it garbled a number or a word, both models sometimes replaced it with the medically plausible version — inventing a fact and attaching a genuine-looking quote to it. This is the hardest failure to catch, because the result looks more correct, not less. Details in the third finding.

How the test works

In plain terms, for readers who haven't seen an AI evaluation before.

The concern with any AI that writes into a medical record is simple: it can produce something that reads perfectly and is wrong. You cannot check that by looking at the output and deciding it seems fine — plausible-looking mistakes are exactly the ones that survive a quick read. So the test is built to be mechanical:

  1. We wrote an answer key by hand. Listening to the round, we listed the facts a correct note must contain for each patient — the blood pressure, the drug, the scan that was ordered — plus a list of facts that were never said, which the system must therefore never write.
  2. We ran the round through the system twice — once with the patient-splitting step on, once with it off — so the two can be compared directly.
  3. An ordinary computer program compared the output to the answer key. Every number in this report comes from that comparison. No AI grades another AI here. That matters, because the system contains an AI "checker" of its own, and that checker is one of the things being tested — it cannot also be the referee.

What we counted, and why each one matters

Facts captured
How many of the answer-key facts actually made it into the note. A missed drug or scan is a real clinical risk, not a cosmetic miss.
Another patient's data in this chart
Whether one patient's numbers or medications appear in a different patient's record. In a hospital this is the worst thing on the list.
Invented quote
Every fact the system writes must come with a word-for-word quote from the recording. Sometimes the AI produces a quote that was never actually said. The system has an automatic check for this that involves no AI at all — it simply searches the transcript for the quote — and anything that fails is held back for a human to confirm.
Quote doesn't back the number
Subtler. The quote is genuine, but it doesn't actually contain the number or drug name the note claims. The quote looks like evidence without being evidence. The system's automatic check does not catch this today; only this evaluation does.
Silently fixed a mis-hearing
The system rewrote a garbled transcription into the medically plausible version — inventing a fact that was never spoken. See finding 3.
Overwritten entries
How many extracted facts were thrown away when the note was saved, because two entries competed for the same slot in the record.

Results with patient-splitting off

The old behaviour. This is the comparison that justifies the new step.

The two models break in opposite directions

Haiku merged everyone. It produced one note with 33 entries covering all four patients at once. Ten slots in the record received multiple competing entries — blood pressure four times over, medications four times over — and because a record slot holds one value, 19 of the 33 entries were silently discarded when the note was saved. Every patient's chart contained four pieces of someone else's clinical data.

Opus dropped everyone but the first. It produced a short, coherent, well-evidenced 11-entry note — for Yossi Cohen alone. It captured 8 of his 8 facts and 0 of Rachel's 7 and 0 of Michael's 9. Three of the four patients on the round are simply absent.

Read Haiku's high "facts captured" score with care — it is an artefact, not a win. Because its single note contains everyone's content, a fact belonging to Rachel can be "found" in text that actually came from Yossi. Contamination inflates the score. That is precisely why mix-ups are counted separately, and why the same note scores 27/30 on facts while carrying 16 mix-ups.

Why Opus's failure is the more dangerous of the two

Haiku's mess is at least visibly a mess — duplicated fields, contradictory vitals, a reviewer would likely notice. Opus's note has no duplicates, no invented quotes, and its internal AI checker marked all 11 entries "properly evidenced." By every signal the system produces, it is a clean note.

It is clean because everything it did write was correct. The problem is everything it didn't write — and nothing in the system detects an omission. A missing patient leaves no trace. A clinician reviewing that note has no way to know that three more people were discussed.

The mis-hearing problem

The finding with the clearest safety implication, present on both models.

The speech-to-text on this recording is rough — it is a real ward round, not a studio reading. Where it produced something garbled, both models sometimes wrote down the version that made medical sense rather than the version that was actually said.

HaikuA blood pressure that was never spoken

The recording, word for word:

יש לנו כאן את יוסי כהן, מספר זהות 1234, יום לאחר קולקטומיה לפרוסקופית, לחץ דם ה-28 ל-78, דופק 82

"…blood pressure the-28 to 78, pulse 82"

What Haiku wrote into the chart:

128/78

The number 128 does not appear anywhere in the recording. The transcription captured 28; the model quietly upgraded it to the plausible 128 and attached a real quote to it.

Note the direction of the harm. A blood pressure of 28 is not survivable, so a faithful reading would have tripped the system's range check and been held for a human. By being helpful, the model routed a fabricated vital sign around the one safeguard designed to catch it.

OpusNaming an organ the doctors didn't name

Opus's version of this is subtler and, to a clinician, more defensible — which is what makes it harder to police. The recording contains a garbled phrase where the injured organ should be. Opus wrote:

בן 34, יומיים לאחר תאונת דרכים, לאחר קרע בטחול בדרגה 2, מטופל שמרנית

"34 years old, two days after a road traffic accident, grade 2 splenic rupture, managed conservatively"

A grade 2 splenic rupture is almost certainly what was meant. But the word "spleen" was never spoken — the audio has an unrelated-sounding phrase in that position. Opus supplied the diagnosis from clinical context. It also silently corrected the spelling of Rachel's operation.

These are good guesses. They may well be right. But a system that writes confident diagnoses the doctors did not say is doing something categorically different from transcription, and the reviewing clinician cannot tell the two apart from the note.

What the safety checks caught — and what they missed

The system has an automatic, non-AI safeguard. Here is how much of the load it carried.

On Haiku it worked. Haiku produced six quotes that were never actually said, usually by stitching together fragments from different parts of the round. The automatic check — a plain text search, no AI involved — caught all six and held them for human confirmation.

On Opus it had nothing to catch — and that is not reassuring. Opus invented zero quotes, so the safeguard never fired. But Opus still made two silent mis-hearing fixes and seven entries where the quote doesn't contain the number being claimed. The better model's mistakes are exactly the ones the current safeguard cannot see.

Concretely: the check asks "does this quote exist in the recording?" It does not ask "does this quote actually contain the number in the note?" On a high-risk medication entry, Opus wrote a dose of 5 mg with a quote that never mentions 5. This evaluation detects that. The live system does not.

What we can and cannot conclude

  • One recording, one run per model. These are not averages. Earlier testing on a different transcript showed genuine run-to-run variation on identical input, so treat single numbers as indicative rather than precise.
  • The answer key needs a clinician's review. Two of the four patients have vitals so garbled that even a human listener cannot recover what was meant. Those are deliberately marked loosely, and a surgeon should check our judgements before these numbers are quoted anywhere.
  • We graded faithfulness, not clinical correctness. The answer key rewards writing what was said — including the transcription's errors — and penalises tidying them up. That is a deliberate choice: a silently corrected value is an invention wearing good evidence. But it means "facts captured" is not the same as "clinically complete".
  • Haiku ran without its reasoning step. That model predates the feature, so its numbers are a floor rather than a like-for-like comparison.

What we recommend

  1. Keep the patient-splitting step. It is the difference between four clean charts and either a contaminated merge or three vanished patients. This is the clearest result in the test.
  2. Strengthen the quote check to verify numbers, not just existence. The logic already exists in this evaluation; moving it into the live safeguard would convert a measurement into an actual control, and would have caught the invented blood pressure.
  3. Add an explicit check that every patient discussed has a chart. Nothing currently detects a missing patient, which is Opus's failure mode and leaves no trace in the output.
  4. Treat the AI checker as a second opinion, not a gate. Across both models it marked almost everything as properly evidenced, including the note that was missing three patients. The non-AI safeguards are what carry the safety argument.
  5. The cheaper model is defensible only because the safeguards hold. Haiku made more errors and the automatic check caught them. That is a genuine cost argument — but it also means the safeguards, not the model, are the product.
